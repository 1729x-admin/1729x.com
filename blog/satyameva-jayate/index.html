सत्यमेव जयते

Truth alone triumphs, and why we begin there.

Most monasteries became the world they renounced. On building one that keeps only what matters.

1729xOct 20, 2026

Four words from the Muṇḍaka Upaniṣad sit beneath the lions on India's national emblem: satyameva jayate, truth alone triumphs. We see them so often on coins and letterheads that we have stopped reading them. Read them again. They do not say truth usually wins, or that truth is one value among many. They say only truth wins.

सत्यमेव जयते नानृतं Truth alone triumphs, not falsehood. — Muṇḍaka Upaniṣad 3.1.6

1729x begins with this sentence. It is the only thing we are built around.

What a monastery was supposed to be

A monastery was meant to be the one place that was not the world. In practice most became exactly what they renounced. They have ranks and titles, schedules and uniforms, rituals performed because they have always been performed, and symbols that stand in for what they once pointed to. They are anxious to recruit, to preserve a lineage and to be seen as holy. Take away the robes and the incense and you find the same hierarchy, ambition and theatre as anywhere else.

This is not a failure of any particular order. It is human nature. The urge to organise, instruct, convert and belong is so deep that it follows us even into the places built to escape it.

A neo-monastery

We want to keep the essence and drop the rest. That means no robes, no rituals, no hierarchy and no curriculum of practices that turn seekers into perpetual students. There is no rulebook, because truth enforced from outside is just another rule. What remains is an atmosphere: a place where saying what is so is the natural thing, where nobody performs, and where nothing false survives for long.

That atmosphere is fragile. It does not survive being diluted, so it has to be protected.

Not for everyone

Most communities measure success by growth. We will not. A place devoted to truth has to be willing to close its door. It is closed to those who come to collect quotes or display their cleverness, to spiritual tourists and to ideologues of every side. It is closed to anyone more interested in appearing wise than in seeing clearly.

The only entry requirement is sincerity: a real, uncomfortable love of truth above one's own image. Degrees, titles and followers count for nothing.

Not a retreat

The old monastery withdrew from the world. We don't. We want to build in it, trade in it, invent in it and create value in it, because reality is the hardest test of truth. A business either serves people or it fails. A bridge either stands or it falls. Markets and engineering are unforgiving teachers, and we respect them. Capitalism, for all its flaws, has lifted more people out of poverty than any sermon. Despising it is often just a way of avoiding its verdict.

So a neo-monastery is not a place to escape life. It is a way of living all of it, including work, wealth and ambition, without lying to ourselves.

Knowledge as sacred

The gurukul treated knowledge as sacred, and we want to recover that. But sacredness does not come from age, lineage or ceremony. It comes from guarding something. Knowledge becomes sacred when it is not cheapened, not traded for applause and not handed to those who will only wear it.

And Ramanujan?

Srinivasa Ramanujan had no degree. He was a clerk in Madras who filled notebooks with results that would occupy mathematicians for a century. The truth was in him all along. Every institution of his day would have turned him away, and one letter that reached the right reader changed that.

That is why our name begins with his number. A place devoted to truth must be closed to pretence and open to the real thing wherever it appears, with or without credentials. The x in 1729x stands for those not yet found.

This journal is where we will think out loud. We will often be wrong, and we intend to find out.

Satyameva jayate.
